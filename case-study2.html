UX research case study, web accessibility

Signing up without asking for help

How research with low-vision and screen reader users helped a disability recreation nonprofit fix the website its own participants couldn't use.

Organization
Northern Illinois Special Recreation Association (NISRA)
Role
UX Researcher
Timeline
May to August 2025
Team
Marketing Director, Web Developer
1

Overview

NISRA runs recreation programs for people with disabilities. Its website was meant to be the front door for sign-up, but small gray text, low contrast and unclear form fields meant many low-vision participants couldn't read program details or register on their own.

The barrier contradicted NISRA's mission of inclusion, and it had a measurable cost: people who wanted to join gave up or relied on a family member to register for them.

Research question

How might we help users with visual impairments read content, navigate pages and register for programs without frustration or assistance?

Why it matters beyond users: the Department of Justice's ADA Title II rule sets WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the standard for state and local government websites, and in April 2026 it extended the compliance deadlines by one year. Special recreation associations are formed by public park districts, which makes this standard directly relevant to organizations like NISRA.

Three barriers

  1. Text too smallProgram details were set at 9 to 11px, with no way to enlarge them on the page.
  2. Contrast too lowGray text on light gray measured 2.97:1, below the 4.5:1 minimum.
  3. Unclear labels and missing alt textFields labeled "Contact" and "Enter here" gave screen reader users no guidance, and images announced only file names.

    I ran 10 one-on-one sessions of 45 to 60 minutes over Zoom with current and prospective NISRA users who have low vision or use assistive technology. Participants were recruited through NISRA staff referrals.

    Each session had two parts

    1. Interview: "Have you tried signing up for a program online? What happened?" and "Do you use a screen reader or other tools? Did they work on this site?"
    2. Observed tasks: find a program, read its details, then try to register alone, using whatever tools they normally use.

    What I saw

    • Participants leaned in, zoomed the whole browser and still lost their place.
    • Screen readers skipped or misread form fields.
    • Most participants stopped and said they'd ask a family member.

    Findings

    2.97:1

    Low contrast made content unreadable

    Body text (#8A8A8A on #EEEEEE) and every other audited pairing fell below 4.5:1. Participants described "straining" and "guessing."

    7 of 10

    Small text forced people to rely on others

    Text of 9 to 11px with no resize control meant 7 of 10 participants needed a caregiver's help to read program details.

    4 of 10

    Vague labels blocked independent sign-up

    Only 4 of 10 completed the form alone. Screen readers announced "edit text" with no label, and images read out file names.

    Recommendations and solution

    Each recommendation answered one finding and was tested with the same participants before launch. Try them below.

    Text resize control

    Resize text
    Weekly Programs. Fall programs meet Monday to Friday. All ability levels, ages 5 and up.

    Answers finding 2. A labeled control in the header, so users don't need to know browser zoom shortcuts.

    Stronger contrast

    Before2.97:1#8A8A8A
    After7.06:1#4F4F4F

    Answers finding 1. Body text now exceeds the 4.5:1 AA minimum and meets the 7:1 enhanced (AAA) level. Navigation and buttons moved to darker teal and green so white text passes too.

    Clear labels and alt text

    Email address (required)
    your.email@example.com
    We'll send your confirmation here.

    Answers finding 3. Labels connected to each field in code, helper text, required fields marked in words, and descriptive alt text on every image.

    Challenges and learnings

    ChallengeWhat I didResult
    Stakeholders saw accessibility as optionalConnected barriers to lost sign-ups and the mission, and used the low-vision simulation in the readoutAccessibility fixes moved to the top of the redesign
    Requests for more colorful imagery over structural fixesReframed the discussion around independence and legal standardsEffort went to contrast, labels and alt text first
    No budget for paid toolsUsed free tools: WebAIM, WAVE and browser dev toolsFixed every contrast, label and alt text failure found in the audit

    Include disabled users from day one

    The audit told me what failed. Watching participants told me what it cost them, and what to fix first.

    Show, don't cite

    Leadership responded to seeing the page through low vision far more than to a list of ratios.

    Re-check the fix

    Testing found new issues in my own redesign. "Done" meant participants succeeded, not that the mockup looked right.

    Next steps

    • Audit against WCAG 2.2, including keyboard navigation and visible focus, which this project didn't cover.
    • Run a quarterly accessibility check as new programs are posted.
    • Add a way for users to report accessibility problems directly.
    • Train staff who post content to write alt text and use accessible colors.

    Closing

    NISRA's website was excluding the people it was built to serve. Three changes made the biggest difference: a text resize control, stronger contrast, and clear labels with alt text. Together they doubled independent sign-up completion in testing, from 40% to 80%.

    The lesson I carry forward: include people with disabilities in research from the start. Everything else follows from that.
