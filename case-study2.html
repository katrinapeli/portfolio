Contrast 2.97:1 FAILED - Text Too Small

Overview

NISRA provides recreation programs for individuals with disabilities, but their website failed basic accessibility standards. Small text, poor contrast, and unclear forms prevented visually impaired users from registering independently - creating barriers that directly contradicted NISRA's mission of inclusion.

Problem Statement
"How might we improve website accessibility so users with visual impairments can read content, navigate pages, and complete tasks without frustration or assistance?"

The Problem

1
Small Text Size
Users with low vision couldn't read program details or form fields, requiring assistance to navigate basic tasks.
2
Poor Color Contrast
Text blended into backgrounds, failing WCAG standards (below 4.5:1 ratio). Users described it as "straining to read."
3
Unclear Form Labels & Missing Alternative Text
Sign-up fields lacked descriptive labels and images had no alt text, making the site effectively unusable for screen reader users.

Key Insights from Interviews

1
Text was unreadable

Small font sizes and low contrast made content inaccessible. Participants reported needing someone else to read the screen for them.

2
Forms were confusing and incomplete

Missing and vague form labels caused errors and abandonment. Screen readers couldn't interpret the fields, leaving assistive technology users with no guidance.

3
Loss of independence and confidence

Users consistently needed caregiver assistance to complete basic tasks. Many gave up entirely. Images lacked alt text, excluding screen reader users from visual content.

4
Screen reader compatibility issues

Poor semantic HTML structure caused assistive technologies to misread or skip content entirely, making navigation unpredictable.

Key Findings

Based on the research, these are the three main pain points identified across sessions.

1
Low contrast made content unreadable.

Text color #8A8A8A on background #EEEEEE measured 2.97:1 - failing the 4.5:1 WCAG AA minimum. All four audited elements failed. Users called it invisible.

2
Small text forced users to rely on others.

Font sizes of 9-10px were too small for low-vision users to read independently. 70% needed caregiver help. There was no built-in text resize option.

3
Unclear labels and no alt text blocked screen reader users.

Vague field labels and missing image alt text made the sign-up form and page content unusable with a screen reader. Participants called it "a guessing game."

Key Learnings

👂

Listen to Users with Disabilities First

Designing for accessibility without talking to people with disabilities first leads to guesswork. Watching real users struggle with things I assumed were simple was the most important part of this project.

🤝

Translate Research to Action

Stakeholders needed business impact, not just user stories. Connecting accessibility to sign-up metrics made the difference.

🔄

Iterate Based on Feedback

Usability testing revealed issues I missed. Embracing that "done" meant iterating until users succeeded, not defending my first solution.

Next Steps

📊 Regular Monitoring

Conduct quarterly accessibility audits to maintain WCAG compliance as the site evolves.

🎯 Expand Accessibility

Address keyboard navigation, focus indicators, and cognitive accessibility.

💬 User Feedback Loop

Implement a mechanism for users to report accessibility issues directly.

👨‍💼 Staff Training

Educate content creators on maintaining accessibility when adding new programs.

Conclusion

NISRA's website was excluding the very people it was built to serve. This project fixed that by listening to users first, then designing based on what they actually needed.

Three changes made the biggest difference: a text resize control, improved color contrast, and clear form labels with alt text. Together, they raised independent sign-up completion from 40% to 80% and increased program enrollment by 20%.

The most important lesson: include people with disabilities in your research from the start. Everything else follows from that.
