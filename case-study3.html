Overview

I evaluated NISRA's existing live mobile site to identify navigation and information architecture issues. Based on research findings, I created a revised sitemap and low-fidelity Figma prototype, validated it with users, and handed off recommendations to the Marketing Director and Web Developer for implementation planning.

The Problem

NISRA's live mobile site made it difficult for users to find programs, register for activities, and locate volunteer information. Overlapping labels, buried content, and unclear categories caused users to click through multiple sections before finding what they needed.

1
Duplicate Menu Labels
"Programs," "Programs & Events," and "Special Events" all existed. Users didn't know which section to choose.
2
Key Content Was Buried
Summer Camps, Registration, Accessibility Info, and Volunteer Opportunities required too many clicks from the main menu.
3
Scattered Content
Forms, registration, and volunteer information were spread across unrelated sections, making key tasks harder to complete.

Optimal Workshop 12 Participants Recruited via Google Forms

Found: Users expected programs grouped together, forms under Resources, and volunteer content in its own section.

3. Moderated User Observations

Zoom 10 Participants Recruited via Google Forms

Participants screen-shared while completing tasks on the live mobile site. I documented where they clicked, hesitated, backtracked, or stopped searching.

Found: Summer Camp and Accessibility Info required 4+ clicks. Users couldn't predict what lived under "Date/Forms" or "About Us." Several stopped after 3-4 unsuccessful attempts. I also noted accessibility barriers: small tap targets, vague link labels, dense content blocks, and Accessibility Info buried 4 levels deep. These were included in the IA recommendations. A formal WCAG audit is recommended before implementation.

Revised Information Architecture

Based on research findings, I restructured the site from 5 sections into 4 clearer categories, renamed unclear labels, and elevated high-priority content. I built a revised sitemap and Figma prototype, then handed both off for implementation planning.

Prototype Validation Testing

After creating the revised Figma prototype, I tested the new structure using two methods. Tree testing measured whether users selected the correct category without backtracking. First-click testing measured whether a user's first tap went in the right direction.

Key Learnings

Users Group Content Differently Than Assumed
Card sorting showed users expected clearer labels and fewer categories. "Community" meant nothing to someone looking for volunteer work.
Testing Before Development Saves Rework
Validating the Figma prototype before handoff gave the team a research-backed direction rather than a structural guess.
Simpler Navigation Outperforms More Features
Consolidating labels and reducing menu items had more impact than adding anything new. Clarity is a design decision.

What I'd Do Differently

  • Recruit more participants with disabilities - future rounds would prioritize users with assistive technology experience
  • Track behavior after implementation - heatmaps and click analytics to confirm whether Figma prototype findings hold on the live site

Next Steps

📊 Track Behavior After Implementation

If implemented, use heatmaps and click analytics to confirm whether prototype findings hold on the live site.

♿ WCAG Accessibility Audit

Conduct a formal accessibility audit before implementation covering tap targets, link labels, contrast, and screen reader support.

🎯 Expand Participant Recruiting

Recruit more users with disabilities and assistive technology experience for future validation rounds.

Conclusion

This project showed how UX research and information architecture testing can improve navigation clarity before development. I evaluated NISRA's original live mobile site, created and tested a revised Figma prototype, and handed off research-backed IA recommendations for implementation planning.

Prototype testing showed satisfaction increased from 42% on the original live-site experience to 85% on the revised Figma prototype, and average task success increased from 52% to 83% across four tree testing tasks. These results gave the team a validated direction for simplifying mobile navigation and improving access to high-priority content.
