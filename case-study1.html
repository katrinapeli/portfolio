UX research case study, clinical workflow

Charting between patients

How behavioral health nurses and technicians document unit events in an EHR, and how the workflow could fit the way a shift actually runs.

Role
UX Researcher (solo)
Timeline
3 weeks
Methods
Interviews, contextual inquiry, concept evaluation, usability testing
Participants
8 simulated staff profiles
Users
RNs, behavioral health techs, social work, charge nurse
Tools
Figma, Google Sheets, AI-assisted coding with manual review
1

The problem

On an inpatient behavioral health unit, staff document observations, incidents, interventions and PRN medications throughout the shift. A single event, such as a patient becoming agitated and receiving a PRN, has to be recorded in several parts of the chart.

When documentation competes with patient safety, safety wins. Notes get started, abandoned and rebuilt from memory at the end of the shift. That affects more than staff time: late or missing PRN reassessments mean the next nurse can't tell whether a medication worked.

Research question

Which steps in documenting a unit event slow staff down, why, and what would let them chart closer to the moment it happens without losing required information?

I framed the question around steps and causes rather than "make it faster," so the research could show which parts of the workflow actually cost time.

Research plan

I wrote a one-page research brief with a product manager, a UX designer and a clinical informatics nurse (roles simulated for this project). We agreed on what decisions the research needed to inform, then listed the team's assumptions so we could test them instead of building on them.

Assumptions we tested

Staff have trouble finding the right place to document an event.

6 of 8 described searching the note list; observed in all 4 inquiry sessions.

Confirmed

The main cost is typing the narrative.

Writing took 38% of task time. Navigating, re-entering and fixing errors took the rest.

Not supported

Interruptions cause lost work.

5 of 8 had lost a note; only 1 of 6 resumed a note in baseline testing.

Confirmed

Staff want fewer required fields.

They accepted the fields. They wanted to see them before signing, not after.

Contextual inquiry

In four walkthrough sessions, staff documented the same scenario: a patient becomes agitated in the day room, staff verbally de-escalate, and the nurse gives a PRN. I timed each step and noted where people hesitated, backtracked or left the screen.

4

Synthesis

I coded interview and walkthrough notes, then grouped them into themes on an affinity board. Filtering by role showed that some problems were shared and some belonged to one group: PRN follow-up was entirely a nursing problem, while note-type confusion came mostly from techs.

How I used AI in synthesis

AI tools sped up the first pass, but every code was checked against the source notes. I kept judgment calls, like severity and recommendations, with the research.

1

De-identify
Removed names, units and dates before any notes went into a tool.

2

First-pass coding
An AI assistant proposed 23 codes from the de-identified notes.

3

I checked each code against the original quote, dropped 6, split 2 and merged the rest into 6 themes.

4

Decide
Severity, confidence and recommendations were written by me, not generated.

Prioritizing opportunities

In a working session with product and design, we scored each opportunity for user impact (from frequency and severity in the research) and effort (from engineering estimates). The concept focused on the top-left quadrant first.

    Workflow before and after

    Current

    8 steps, 6.2 min
    1. Open patient chart
    2. Find the right note type
    3. Write event narrative
    4. Go to MAR to record PRN
    5. Re-enter time, location and staff in incident form
    6. Update observation flowsheet
    7. Sign, hit required-field error, go back
    8. Set own reminder for PRN reassessment

    Concept

    5 steps, 3.4 min
    1. Start "Unit event" from the patient banner
    2. Pick event type (time, location, staff prefill)
    3. Write what happened and check interventions
    4. Confirm the PRN pulled from the MAR
    5. Review inline requirements and sign; reassessment reminder is set automatically

    Usability testing

    I ran moderated sessions with 6 participants (3 RNs, 3 techs) on a baseline prototype of the current workflow, then two rounds on the concept. Each session used the same three tasks, written in clinical language without naming any buttons.

    1. Document an event. "A patient just became agitated in the day room and you gave a PRN. Document what happened."
    2. Resume after an interruption. Mid-task, the moderator calls the participant away for 2 minutes, then asks them to finish.
    3. Record PRN follow-up. "It's now an hour later. Record whether the PRN was effective."

    What I measured

    MetricWhy
    Time on taskDirect cost to a shift
    Task completionWhether the note was signed with required fields
    Errors per sessionWrong note type, missing fields, backtracking
    SEQ (1–7)Perceived ease right after each task
    SUS (0–100)Overall usability, compared with the 68 benchmark

    What changed between rounds

    Unfinished notes

    Round 1: drafts saved but lived in a menu; 2 of 6 didn't find them.

    Round 2: a badge on the patient banner. 6 of 6 resumed.

    Event types

    Round 1: a list of 14 types slowed selection by about 20 seconds.

    Round 2: 5 common types as chips, the rest under "More types."

    PRN link

    Round 1: the "Attach order" label confused 3 of 6 participants.

    Round 2: renamed to "PRN linked from MAR," with the reminder shown inline.

    Research operations

    So the next study doesn't start from a blank page, I turned this project's documents into reusable templates and logged every insight in a lightweight repository that product and design can search.

    Reusable templates

    Background
    Staff report that documenting unit events takes too long and often happens at the end of shift.
    Decision to inform
    Which parts of event documentation to redesign first for the next release.
    Research questions
    1. Where does time go when documenting an event?
    2. What causes notes to be delayed or abandoned?
    3. Which required information do staff struggle to provide, and why?
    Assumptions
    Listed with the evidence that would confirm or reject each.
    Methods and n
    Interviews (8), contextual inquiry (4), usability testing (2 × 6).
    Participants
    RNs, techs, social work, charge nurse; mix of shifts and tenure.
    Timeline
    Week 1 interviews and inquiry; week 2 synthesis and concept; week 3 testing and readout.
    Warm-up (5 min)
    Tell me about your role and a typical shift on the unit.
    Recent event (15 min)
    • Walk me through the last time a patient had an event you documented.
    • Where were you when you started the note? Where did you finish it?
    • What happened between starting and finishing?
    Workarounds (10 min)
    • Do you keep any notes outside the EHR? Tell me about that.
    • What do you do when you're not sure which note type to use?
    Medication follow-up (10 min)
    How do you keep track of PRN reassessments during a busy shift?
    Wrap-up (5 min)
    If you could change one thing about documenting events, what would it be?
    Intro
    "We're testing the system, not you. Please think out loud."
    Task wording
    Written as a clinical scenario. No button or menu names in the prompt.
    Success criteria
    Note signed with all required fields, correct event type, PRN linked.
    Measures
    Time on task, completion, errors, SEQ after each task, SUS at the end.
    Interruption protocol
    Moderator calls the participant away at a set point for 2 minutes, then says "Please finish your note."
    Debrief
    What felt different from how you chart today?
    Finding
    One sentence describing what we saw.
    Evidence
    Counts, task metrics and 1–2 representative quotes.
    Impact
    Effect on staff time, patient safety, compliance or handoff.
    Opportunity
    A direction to explore, not a finished design.
    Confidence
    High, medium or low, with the reason (sample size, method, role coverage).

    Reflection

    What I learned

    The biggest shift came from timing the steps. Everyone, including me, assumed the narrative was the slow part. The data said navigation and re-entry were, which changed what we designed.

    Staff didn't want less documentation. They wanted to know what was required up front and to trust that their work wouldn't disappear when a patient needed them.

    Limitations

    This is a simulated project. Participants, quotes and numbers are illustrative, built from my experience in behavioral health. Sample sizes are small, and prototype timing won't match a live EHR with real latency and logins.

    What I'd do next

    • Validate the themes with real staff across day, evening and night shifts.
    • Interview the admin and clinical informatics users who configure note types and required fields, since they shape what frontline staff see.
    • Pair qualitative findings with product analytics: time from event to signed note, notes signed after shift end, and late PRN reassessments.
    • Run a concept test on bedside charting with tablets, the highest-effort opportunity we parked.
